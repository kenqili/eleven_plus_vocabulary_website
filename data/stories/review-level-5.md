# Level 5 editorial review

This is an AI-generated editorial simulation using two age-based reading perspectives, not feedback from real children. No children were contacted, and enjoyment has not been established through user testing.

## Simulated 9-year-old boy perspective

Reviewed for an immediate problem, physical comedy, clear cause and effect, and a payoff worth reading to the end. Potential sticking points were the long animal adjectives in story 1 and the several similar behaviour words in story 9. Each animal adjective now points to a visible costume part, and the animal costume has a practical goal: include every younger child's contribution. In story 9, the argument changes into a specific cake-sharing problem and solution. Humour comes from situations (a supposedly walking soup, a recent jam-tart 'fossil', a cushion train), not from mocking someone for struggling with reading.

## Simulated 10-year-old girl perspective

Reviewed for meaningful contributions from different characters, fair outcomes, believable dialogue, and vocabulary understandable from its sentence. Avoided making girls invariably careful helpers and boys invariably boastful or careless: the biscuit entrepreneur Alex is a girl who learns to listen to her friend Sam, while other stories give girls and boys different creative and problem-solving roles. Replaced a contrived sharp-stick moment in the puppet story with a prop fork retrieving a paper speech. The lesson is delivered through a funny burp, not a safety lecture. Added clear motivation to the mixed costume and a genuinely useful record in the museum mystery.

## Word-sense and clarity checks

- Read all 146 source definitions before drafting. Every level 5 word appears in at least one story.
- Scientific animal adjectives identify the correct species; `asinine` is used in its literal donkey-related sense with an explicit explanation.
- `ambulatory` appears as an intentionally silly walking-soup joke whose meaning is stated. `palaeontologist` concerns fossils, not historical human objects; the museum `artefact` is a human-made spoon.
- `conflagration` refers to a large destructive historical fire represented in a model, not a candle or a harmless spark.
- `intermittently` describes a faulty machine starting and stopping; `sporadically` describes irregular visits.
- `apprehend` is clearly explained as arrest, while `apprehension` describes worry. `impale` means piercing paper with a fork, and `dilate` describes pupils widening in shade.
- Similar difficult words receive separate concrete contexts rather than a string of synonyms.
- The wrongdoing story discusses behaviour and repair; it does not endorse labelling children as bad people.

## Structural validation

Ten stories, each 350–550 words and 15–16 unique level-matched target words. All 146 level 5 words are covered, with a small amount of deliberate repetition. Every exact target occurrence in story paragraphs is bolded, and every bold marker is a listed target. Each story has a three-option comprehension question, with the answer position varied. This validation checks structure and coverage; actual child reading sessions remain useful future research.

## Independent second pass

Reread the complete level after reviewing the other forty stories. Corrected the boat description so the water **level**, rather than water as a substance, fluctuates. Corrected the museum mystery's physical layout: the toy goat is on the ledge outside the glass case, so a visiting toddler could actually leave it there. Confirmed that the ten comprehension answers are supported by the narratives. The simulated reading perspectives found no remaining material clarity, word-sense or plot-consistency blockers after these revisions; this remains an editorial judgement, not evidence of real children's preferences.
