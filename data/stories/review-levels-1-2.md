# Editorial review: levels 1 and 2

These are AI-authored stories and simulated editorial reviews. No real child read, approved, rated or tested these stories. The perspectives below are writing checks, not evidence that children will enjoy the stories. A supervised trial with actual readers would still be useful.

## Simulated reader perspectives

- **Imagined nine-year-old boy:** checked whether the opening provides something concrete to picture, the middle contains a problem worth following, and the ending pays off a joke or clue. Favoured the stone pets, sock captain, sneezing volcano, toy parrot and multiple moustaches. Checked that target vocabulary does not become a sequence of unrelated events.
- **Imagined ten-year-old girl:** checked whether both characters contribute, an unfamiliar word has enough surrounding context, jokes avoid humiliating somebody, and a reader can answer the final question from the story. Favoured the garden team's practical solution, miniature museum, puppet rewrite and shared playground plan. Checked that boys and girls can both invent, lead, care, perform and solve problems.

These are imagined individual preferences, not claims about what either gender generally likes.

## Revisions and checks

- Kept dialogue, visual jokes and concrete goals around more abstract words such as `vanity`, `potential`, `apathy` and `discord`.
- Reworked the community puppet story so `obscene` belongs to the discussion of the performance, removing an unrelated dictionary-exercise aside. Humour targets the rabbit's hat, while grief, poverty, famine and religion are handled respectfully.
- Clarified that the museum desert is a model and its mirage is an illustration; a real mirage is an optical effect. Separated model-volcano bubbles from real magma and molten rock.
- Kept first-aid learning focused on seeking adult help. Practical science and physical challenge activities include appropriate adult supervision within the narrative.
- Made the biscuit mystery's instruction concrete: look for evidence before deciding. The answer follows from the sloping shelf and trail of clues.
- Checked idioms in context: `break a leg` means good luck, `crack up` means laugh, and `resort to` introduces a last option. Used `wax` to mean grow, `hawk` to mean sell, `hind` as a female deer, `beck` in the familiar phrase, and `proxy` as an authorised representative.
- Rotated comprehension-answer positions to avoid a repeated first-option pattern. Questions test the central cause or solution rather than obscure recall.

## Automated validation

Each level has exactly ten stories. Every story has 15 distinct target words from its assigned level, between 350 and 550 words, four readable paragraphs, and a three-option comprehension question with a valid answer index. Each level covers all 146 vocabulary IDs, with four intentional repeated word assignments to reach 150 target slots. All target occurrences in story paragraphs are marked with `**` and no non-target text is bolded. Definitions were read from the merged word bank before drafting and checked against the intended senses.

## Second editorial pass and satisfaction verdict

A separate agent reread all twenty stories and flagged three awkward expressions and excessive explanatory interruptions in some adventures. Those issues were revised: `cuisine` now has a natural singular construction, the garden shows a substantial improvement, and `myriad` modifies the possible decorations directly. The soup's blunt knife now spreads butter rather than seeming to chop the missing vegetables. The drama ending straightens the crooked frame instead of referring to unexplained damage.

Repeated reminders that the museum and drama adventures were simulated were replaced with details that advance the scene: the dragon's breakfast mug, a trail of floury footprints, a nose-shaped cave and a villain hiding behind an undersized houseplant. The museum/play settings remain clear, and essential adult roles in practical activities remain intact.

**Simulated nine-year-old-boy perspective: satisfied with the revised editorial draft.** The central problems have visible goals, the clues pay off, and each ending contains a specific comic image or callback. The shorter interruptions help the adventures keep moving.

**Simulated ten-year-old-girl perspective: satisfied with the revised editorial draft.** Both partners contribute to solutions, difficult vocabulary has usable context, the jokes do not depend on humiliating vulnerable characters, and the questions can be answered from the story. No unresolved accuracy or plot issue was identified in this pass.

These verdicts describe AI editorial checks against imagined reading preferences. They are not ratings, testimonials or proof of enjoyment from actual children. Automated coverage, level membership, word count, bold-occurrence and question checks passed again after the revisions.

The independent final check also caught noun `yield` against the source bank's verb definition. The garden now "would yield more carrots", matching the hover definition exactly. Priya now joins in the grand soup-name game, and Nia supplies the saucepan-dressing-room joke, giving girls more of the comic invention as well as sensible solutions.

The volcano comprehension prompt was also made actor-neutral: "Why is the model labelled ‘mostly newspaper’?" Ravi writes that label in the story, so the previous attribution to Hana was removed. The independent reviewer found the revised prose livelier and reported editorial satisfaction after these corrections.
