# Editorial review: levels 3 and 4

These are original fictional stories reviewed using two simulated reader perspectives: a nine-year-old boy who enjoys visual jokes and active mysteries, and a ten-year-old girl who enjoys believable friendships, puzzles and satisfying endings. These are editorial exercises performed by an AI, not interviews, child testing, quotations from children or evidence that real children enjoyed the stories. The perspectives do not imply that either preference belongs to one gender.

## Revisions following the simulated passes

- Renamed the soup story to **The Soup with a Bubble Hat**, matching the actual comic incident rather than promising a tent-climbing event that never happens.
- Kept the library mystery grounded in observable clues: radiator noise, a vibrating pipe and a sound-button toy. The extra treasure-hunt clues are explicitly explained rather than left dangling.
- Added a football-sticker joke to the museum's probate/probation exhibit to break up its more demanding explanation. Both meanings remain distinct and match the word bank.
- Removed an awkward in-story grammar rehearsal from **King Boots and the Missing Throne**. The king now naturally proclaims the shared feast.
- Checked that both boys and girls make discoveries, admit mistakes, help friends and get funny lines. No character's intelligence or interest is explained by nationality or gender.
- Gave the prank story a clear repair: the insult is explicitly challenged, the organisers apologise without demanding forgiveness, and the affected children help design the improved game. The difficult target word “imbecile” is presented as a hurtful insult, never as the narrator's judgement of a child.
- Kept the mouse-hotel discussion concrete, with paper mice, two doors, an inspection hatch and a tiny manager. The health words are used in an informational context rather than making a child's illness the joke.
- Checked endings for payoffs: the goat's short acceptance speech, the duck's retirement sign, the paper-mouse hotel review and the wrongly accused dog's final biscuit mystery all return to earlier details.
- Varied the correct-answer positions across the three comprehension options; no trick questions or required outside knowledge.

## Word-sense review

All target definitions were read from the existing word bank before drafting. Particular checks included: **complement/compliment**, **probate/probation**, **obtuse** (angle), **sanction** (permission), **flog** (sell), **vapour**, **polarity** (opposing moods), **incision** (cut), **platonic** (friendship), **murine**, **censure**, **absolve**, **infamy/notoriety**, and **amoral**. Bold text contains only target words from the corresponding difficulty level. Repeated target occurrences in story paragraphs are marked too.

## Mechanical validation

Each file contains exactly ten JSON story records. Every record has a stable ID, level, ordinal, title, summary, target IDs, paragraphs and one three-option comprehension question. Each level covers all 146 of its word-bank entries. Stories contain 15–17 unique target words at level 3 and 15 at level 4, with deliberate repetition between stories where needed. Story body lengths are 375–474 words. Automated checks verify coverage, levels, marker consistency, length, question shape and answer bounds.

A real parent/child reading trial would still provide useful feedback about pace, humour and individual reading confidence.

## Final independent editorial pass

A further complete read used the same two simulated age-nine/age-ten perspectives after the coverage checks. The prank story received one additional revision: “inane” now describes the obviously silly idea of blaming the sock, rather than a teacher dismissing a child's explanation. This preserves the precise vocabulary meaning, adds a small joke and keeps the adult response constructive.

Final simulated-reader verdicts: both perspectives are satisfied that the stories have understandable goals, resolved endings, opportunities to laugh and protagonists worth following. The vocabulary-accuracy pass is also satisfied. The more demanding museum and science passages remain intentionally supported by concrete exhibits, paper models and nearby explanations; hover notes provide further help in the app. There are no outstanding editorial blockers for these two levels. These verdicts are AI editorial judgements, not feedback collected from real children.

## Cross-review revisions

An independent agent read all twenty stories and caught a continuity error in the goat election: the goat now hops through the open gate, consistent with Theo's admission. Following that review, the museum trail now has a six-stamp goal and a final envelope payoff; the space story replaces an unnecessary electrical-safety aside with a moon-rock crumb joke; and the mouse hotel replaces repeated care warnings and a bare definition with concrete paper guests, a recovering mouse and a room-service bell. Vocabulary meaning and the full target coverage are preserved.

The independent reviewer reread those changes and confirmed satisfaction from simulated age-nine girl/age-ten boy and adult accuracy perspectives. Their final minor request was also applied: the space story now explicitly states that the lights are switched off between rehearsals, directly supporting its comprehension answer. No requested editorial changes remain outstanding.
